# Editor disposition (23 August 2026)

The objections memorandum had never had a native-ear pass. At roughly 76 000
characters per language it was too large for one bundle, which is why it was
skipped in every earlier sweep. Split into five chunks by objection group, it
ran: twenty reviews, four languages, all twenty-one objections.

## What came back

261 findings. The triage that matters is not their number but their spread:
250 are local phrasings that occur once, and 11 recur across the corpus. That
is a good result about the terminology and a large one about the prose. The
translations are consistent; they read as translations.

## The policy applied, and why it is narrower than the findings

Fixes were applied where the target text is **wrong**, and withheld where it
is merely **not native**. Wrong means a false friend that misleads a lawyer, a
collocation that does not exist, a construction that is ungrammatical, or a
metaphor that makes a legal file sound unserious. Not-native means accurate
prose that reads as translated.

The reason for the line is that every edit to text which has already passed a
fidelity gate can move the meaning, and this pass proved that risk is real
rather than theoretical, five times, in the same session.

## The loop, and what it caught

Five stages, each of them: apply the native-ear fixes, re-run the fidelity
gate on that chunk against the English, fix whatever the fidelity round shows
the fixes introduced. The re-run is not a formality. It caught **five defects
of my own**, in four of the five stages:

- Spanish *autoridades* (feminine) changed to *precedentes* (masculine),
  leaving *las invocará*, *cada una*, *unívoca* and *enunciada* agreeing with a
  noun that was no longer there. Two BLOCKING, the identical error to the
  French and German agreement bug of 21 August.
- A Dutch clause left without its main verb, after the finite verb was moved
  out and not put back.
- German word order broken in a main clause after *und*, producing a
  subordinate-clause verb-final where the main clause needed verb-second.
- A German comma splice, from replacing a verb phrase without re-coordinating
  the clause it sat in.
- A German roadmap sentence forked from the four subheadings it announces:
  I replaced *Perimeter und Ausgang* on a native-ear note, but the English
  roadmap itself says "the perimeter and the exit" and the headings below are
  *Dann der Perimeter* and *Zuletzt der Ausgang*. That one was pure over-reach
  and was reverted rather than propagated.

## What the fidelity re-runs found that predates this pass

Worse than anything native-ear surfaced, and the reason the loop was worth
running at all:

- **Dutch reversed an anti-avoidance trigger.** Article 5(11) fires where
  automated assets *leave at less than arm's length*; the Dutch said they
  leave *onder zakelijke voorwaarden*, which is at arm's length. The rule
  fired on exactly the transfers it is meant to permit. BLOCKING.
- **German ran the argument out of order.** The passage opens by promising to
  close the timing, the ranking, the perimeter and the exit and then be honest
  where the closure is partial. The German put the leverage carve-out before
  source taxation, so the honest partial no longer came last. Verified against
  the English before touching it; fixed as a pure block swap, no word changed.
- French rendered *statutory events* as **événements statutaires**, which in
  French means the company's own articles of association: the text said
  crystallisation is triggered by events the company defines for itself, which
  reverses DC-3.
- German and Spanish both dropped the substantive legal standard from Article
  5(10), stating that subordinating arrangements are ineffective without the
  *main purpose or effect* test that decides when they are.
- Dutch justified Booker Aquaculture on a public-health emergency; the case
  was animal health.

## Forks in defined terms, which were the recurring defect

Nine places where one concept had two renderings inside the same document. Each
was resolved to the variant the corpus already used most, never to the
reviewer's preference where that would have introduced a third:

| language | concept | was | now |
|---|---|---|---|
| German | crystallisation | Kristallisation 4 / Realisierung 6 | Realisierung |
| German | the legislative file | Datei 4 / Akte 26 | Akte |
| Dutch | crystallisation | omzetting 3 / verzilvering 6 | verzilvering |
| Dutch | the review gates | reviewpoorten 1 / toetsingspoort 3 | toetsingspoort |
| French | the review gates | portes de relecture 1 / portes de contrôle 4 | porte de contrôle |
| French | falsification condition | falsification 2 / réfutation 3 | réfutation |
| French | warrant | bon 2 / warrant 37 | warrant |
| Dutch | heading "what it gets right" | 2 against 19 | the 19 |
| Dutch | heading "design consequence" | 2 against 19 | the 19 |

The German *Datei* case is the one worth remembering: the reviewer proposed
*Dossier*, which would have been a third variant. The document already used
*Akte* twenty-six times. Checking the corpus before accepting a proposed
correction is what kept that from happening, and the same check showed the
*Datei* in about, evidence and versions are real computer files and were left
alone.

## What was checked and deliberately not changed

- **"Failed the gates."** All three of Dutch, French and German were told this
  is a managerial calque. It is not: the English means the project's own six
  review gates, and says so four paragraphs later ("Four review gates rejected
  that"). The literal rendering is correct. Three would-be fixes averted.
- **"Designation and realisation."** Flagged as a stray against the
  crystallisation term in three languages. The English says *realisation* in
  that one sentence and *crystallisation* in the other sixteen; nl, fr and es
  mirror it exactly. Faithful, left alone.
- **Honeypot.** *Honingpot*, *pot de miel*, *tarro de miel*. It is the title
  metaphor of objection 9 and is carried consistently; changing it inside one
  objection would fork the corpus.
- **Dutch *vijandig*** for hostile counsel and hostile reading. Nine uses
  across three files, entirely consistent. Unlike the German
  feindselig/gegnerisch case fixed this morning, nothing has forked here, so
  this is a terminology decision rather than a defect.
- **"Onder artikel" against "krachtens artikel"** in Dutch, 8 against 4. A
  style split, both defensible, recorded for a single decision.

## Recorded for decision, still open

**Covered undertaking against designated.** German, French, Spanish and Dutch
all render *covered undertaking* with the word for *designated*. Five separate
gate runs have now raised it independently, which is the strongest signal any
recorded item has. The source distinguishes them: designation is the act,
covered is the resulting status. Against that, an undertaking becomes covered
precisely by being designated under Article 3(4). It is corpus-wide, it would
touch every translated file, and it is a terminology decision rather than a
defect fix. The evidence has hardened since 21 August; it is now the largest
open translation item.

**Liquidity event.** Flagged in three languages as a finance-English calque.
A defined term used consistently across the plain layer, the brief,
severability and objections. Unchanged.

**Evidence base.** *Bewijs* / *Preuves* / *base de evidencia*. A document-level
term question, not confined to objections, and left for a pass of its own.
