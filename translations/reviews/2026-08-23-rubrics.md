# Translated article and annex rubrics (23 August 2026)

Until now the article and annex headings were English on every page in every
language. A Dutch reader met "**Artikel** 3: Designation of covered
undertakings" — the label word localised, the heading not — and the whole
contents list, which is the most-scanned page on the site, was twenty English
lines. That reads as an oversight rather than a policy.

## What is translated and what is not

The rubrics only. The legal text stays English and authoritative, as the
banner on every law page says. The rubrics are comprehension aids, exactly
like the plain-language layer, and they now behave like it.

The page layout follows the principle already stated in LawDoc: *the law block
reads English end to end, whatever the page language*. So the translated
rubric heads the page as its h1, and the English rubric stays inside the law
block, stepped down to h2 and set in the softer ink, directly under the
English eyebrow. The reader gets the heading in their own language and can
still match it against the authentic text. On English pages nothing changed.

## Terminology: taken from the corpus, not invented

The first draft of three rubrics invented phrases for *covered undertakings*
— "onder de regeling vallende ondernemingen", "relevant du règlement",
"sujetas al reglamento". The corpus already renders *covered* as **gedekte /
couverte / erfasste / cubierta** and *designated* as **aangewezen / désignées
/ benannt / designadas**. The invented phrases would have been a fourth
variant of the term this project has spent two days unforking. They were
replaced with the corpus's own words.

This is worth recording for the open covered-against-designated decision:
**the distinction already exists in the corpus.** The plain layer and the
commentary distinguish the two words; it is the objections memorandum that
conflates them. Resolving in favour of distinguishing is therefore the smaller
change, not the larger one. Article 3's rubric is also the one place in the
instrument where both words appear in the same phrase, so it cannot be written
without taking a position.

## What the fidelity gate caught

Round one returned REVISE in all four. Five corrections, four of them real:

- **Dutch had the wrong gender.** *Het* burgerkapitaalwarrant, where the
  corpus says *de warrant* twelve times and *het warrant* never.
- **German would have collided with itself.** *Überwachung und Bewertung* for
  Article 14, where *Bewertung* is already valuation in Article 6 and
  twenty-one times in the corpus. Now *Überwachung und Evaluierung*.
- **German annex titles** used *Methode* where EU acts use *Methodik*, and
  Annex II carried an unreadable extended attribute.
- **Article 10 was singular** in Dutch, French and Spanish where the source is
  plural and the German was already plural. All four now agree.
- French *Comité* became *Procédure de comité*, matching the comitology rubric
  and the German and Spanish forms.

Declined, with reason: the reviewer wanted *le warrant de capital des
citoyens* for symmetry with Article 8. The corpus names the instrument *warrant
de capital citoyen*, and changing the rubric alone would fork the name from
the body text. Internal symmetry is worth less than agreeing with the corpus.

Round two: PUBLISH in nl, de and es; the remaining French finding is the
POLISH above.

## The two guards, both falsified

A translated rubric can go wrong in two silent ways, so both are now gated,
and both gates were proved by breaking them:

1. **Drift.** If a rubric changes in the law repo, a translation of the old
   heading would keep rendering. `sync-law.mjs` compares the English column
   stored beside each translation against the live rubric and refuses to
   build. Proved: renaming Article 13 upstream fails the build; restoring it
   passes.
2. **Absence.** A missing rubric falls back to English, which renders
   perfectly and says nothing. `check-translations` now fails on any
   article or annex without a rubric in any locale. Proved: deleting the
   German Article 16 row fails the gate; restoring it passes.
